A census of Ethereum mainnet

Your account can go post-quantum. The contracts you use mostly can't.

Frame transactions (EIP-8141) are designed to let users switch to quantum-safe signatures. But every permit, signed order, bridge attestation and rollup proof is checked by a contract — and those contracts still verify cryptography a quantum computer can break.

Usage: March 2025 – March 2026 (blocks 22.1M–24.7M). Contract state and balances: November 2026.

Almost all of it is one precompile

We looked for every contract that verifies pre-quantum cryptography inside the EVM: ecrecover (secp256k1 ECDSA), the elliptic-curve precompiles used for SNARKs, BLS, blobs and passkeys, the same curve maths done in software, and RSA. For contrast, we also searched all bytecode for lattice (post-quantum) verifiers.

Most callers are wallets. Most activity is applications.

Wallets are the easy part: their owners can rotate keys or move funds. The rest of this piece looks at the applications — the contracts users depend on but don't control.

Who can fix the applications?

We read the source (or bytecode) of the largest applications, which carry of application value. For each we asked two questions: is there a way to use it without the pre-quantum check, and can the contract change?

Each tile: the contracts in that cell and what they did over the year. Darker means more. "Configurable" means the code is fixed but its keys, signers or verifier address can be changed by an admin.

Who holds the keys matters more than whether the user is quantum-safe

Much of the value isn't authorised by the user at all. Market makers sign quotes, bridge committees sign attestations, oracles sign reports. Those keys sit with operators, and moving them to post-quantum signatures needs new contract code, not just a new key.

What happens when a user migrates

A user can go post-quantum in two ways: open a new account with a PQ key, or keep their address and swap its authorisation logic. How a contract checks signatures decides what each user experiences.

The fix for the migrated-address case is at the protocol level: EIP-8151 makes ecrecover fail for any address that has real code — but only once the account has left EIP-7702 delegation.

The permit problem

permit lets a holder approve a spender with a signature instead of a transaction. In most tokens that signature can only be ECDSA. If the token is immutable, nothing but a protocol change can stop an old key from being used.

Rollups, and the absence of lattices

Zero-knowledge rollups prove their state to L1 with SNARKs over the BN254 curve, and every blob-posting rollup relies on KZG commitments. Most of the SNARK verifiers are standalone contracts the rollup can swap — the cost is that hash-based (STARK) verification is roughly 4–20× more gas.

How this was measured

Contracts
Addresses that, as the executing contract, made a successful call to an elliptic-curve precompile or to modexp with a curve or RSA-sized modulus. For proxies this is the proxy; for EIP-7702 accounts it is the EOA. Taken from a trace of every mainnet transaction.
Active in the year
Made at least one such call between March 2025 and March 2026. "Ever" counts since each precompile went live.
Transactions
Distinct transactions in the year in which at least one of the contracts made such a call. A transaction counts once, however many calls it makes.
Value moved
For each of those transactions, the largest single token or ETH transfer in it, summed. Estimated from a 1-in-10 block sample and priced at the time. It measures economic size, not net flow, and understates bridges, whose attestations usually run in a separate transaction from the transfer.
Held now
ETH and the top 50 tokens the contract holds today. Rollup and bridge escrows are often separate contracts, so this undercounts what a verifier secures.
Reliance, code
Read from verified source or bytecode for the largest contracts; rules (proxy slots, selectors, names) for the long tail. 65 of the 433 hand classifications are low confidence.

Contracts that implement ECDSA in plain Solidity, or weren't called during the year, aren't counted. Full method, caveats and sources are in the report.

Data from a full mainnet precompile trace (precompile-tracer), Xatu, Blockscout, Sourcify, DefiLlama and L2BEAT. Code and data: github.com/kevaundray/pq-chain-analysis.
