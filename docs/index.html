The big picture

Your account can go post‑quantum. The contracts you use mostly can't.

Frame transactions (EIP-8141) are designed to let users switch to quantum-safe signatures. But every permit, signed order, bridge attestation and rollup proof is checked by a contract — and those contracts still verify cryptography a quantum computer can break.

The scale

Almost all of it is one precompile: ecrecover

We counted every contract that verifies pre-quantum cryptography inside the EVM — ECDSA signatures, the elliptic-curve precompiles behind SNARKs, BLS, blobs and passkeys, the same curve maths done in software, and RSA — and searched all deployed bytecode for post-quantum lattice verifiers.

Can it change?

Most applications can't drop pre-quantum cryptography on their own

We read the source or bytecode of the largest applications — of application value — and asked two questions: is there a way in without the pre-quantum check? and can the contract change?

"Configurable" means the code is fixed but an admin can change its keys, signers or verifier address. Stronger colour means more.

Who holds the keys

Much of the value is authorised by operators, not users

Market makers sign quotes, bridge committees sign attestations, oracles sign reports. A user switching to a quantum-safe account changes none of that — those operators have to ship new contract code.

Migration

What happens when a user goes post-quantum

Users can open a new account with a post-quantum key, or keep their address and change how it's authorised. How a contract checks signatures decides what each of them experiences.

The fix for the migrated-address case is at the protocol level: EIP-8151 makes ecrecover fail for any address that has real code — but only once the account has left EIP-7702 delegation.

Tokens

Billions in tokens sit behind ECDSA-only permit

permit approves a spender with a signature instead of a transaction. In most tokens that signature can only be ECDSA, and if the token is immutable, only a protocol change can stop an old key from using it.

Proofs

Rollups lean on curves too — and lattices are nowhere

ZK rollups prove their state to L1 with SNARKs over BN254, and every blob-posting rollup relies on KZG commitments. Most SNARK verifiers are standalone contracts a rollup can swap, at the cost of roughly 4–20× more gas for hash-based (STARK) verification.

Method

How this was measured

Contracts
Addresses that, as the executing contract, made a successful call to an elliptic-curve precompile, or to modexp with a curve or RSA-sized modulus. Proxies count as the proxy; EIP-7702 accounts as the EOA. From a trace of every mainnet transaction.
Active in the year
Made at least one such call between March 2025 and March 2026. "Ever" counts since each precompile went live.
Transactions
Distinct transactions in the year in which at least one of the contracts made such a call. A transaction counts once however many calls it makes.
Value moved
For each of those transactions, the largest single token or ETH transfer in it, summed. Estimated from a 1-in-10 block sample, priced at the time. It understates bridges, whose attestations usually run separately from the transfer.
Held now
ETH plus the top 50 tokens the contract holds today. Rollup and bridge escrows are often separate contracts, so this undercounts what a verifier secures.
Reliance and code
Read from verified source or bytecode for the largest contracts; rules (proxy slots, selectors, names) for the long tail. 65 of the 433 hand classifications are low confidence.

Contracts that implement ECDSA in plain Solidity, or weren't called during the year, aren't counted. Full method, caveats and sources are in the report.
